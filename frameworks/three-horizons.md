# Three Horizons

> First draft by the AI assistant. The worked example uses the illustrative results of the [opportunity scoring model](../tools/opportunity-scoring/), which rest on a fictional company. For the project owner's review.

## What it is

A way to look at an organisation's growth initiatives in three groups, by how far they are from today's business:

| Horizon | What it holds | Typical question |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizon 1** | Extending and defending the core business | How do we do what we do, better? |
| **Horizon 2** | Building emerging businesses that could become a new core | What will earn our money in a few years? |
| **Horizon 3** | Creating options: small bets on ideas that may matter much later | What should we learn about now? |

The model comes from Baghai, Coley and White, *The Alchemy of Growth* (1999). Its central claim is that a company has to work on all three at the same time, because the three need different people, different measures of success and different patience. A company that only works on Horizon 1 has nothing ready when the core weakens.

Two things are often misread:

- **The horizons are about distance from the core, not only about time.** "Short, medium and long term" is a shorthand that hides the point. A Horizon 2 business can be built quickly; a Horizon 1 improvement can take years.
- **The horizons are managed differently.** Horizon 1 is judged by profit and efficiency, Horizon 2 by growth and customers won, Horizon 3 by what was learned for the money spent.

## When to use it, and when not

**Use it** to check the balance of a portfolio after the single initiatives have been evaluated. It answers a question that a ranking cannot: are all our bets of the same kind?

**Do not use it** to choose between initiatives. It has no criteria and no scores. And treat the time dimension with caution: Blank (2019) argues that the link between horizon and time no longer holds, because new businesses can now be built as fast as core improvements. What still holds is the difference in how the three have to be managed.

A different use of the same name exists in futures work (Curry and Hodgson, 2008), where the three horizons describe a system that is in decline, in transition and emerging. It is a tool for discussing change in a sector, not for managing a portfolio.

## How to apply it

1. List the initiatives, each described as a concrete use (not as a technology).
2. For each, ask three questions:
   - Does it serve today's customers with today's products or processes? → Horizon 1
   - Does it create a new source of revenue or a new business model, with a plausible path to scale? → Horizon 2
   - Is it an option whose value depends on things that are still unknown? → Horizon 3
3. Count the initiatives and the money per horizon.
4. Compare the balance with the strategy. A strategy that asks for new revenue cannot be served by a portfolio that is all Horizon 1.
5. Give each horizon its own measure of success and, where possible, its own owner.

## Worked example

The seven opportunities of the [scoring model](../tools/opportunity-scoring/), for a fictional machine builder whose strategy is to grow service revenue from 20% to 30%, raise its margin, and differentiate its machines through digital functions.

| Opportunity | Horizon | Why | Class in the scoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital twin for virtual commissioning | 1 | Makes the core engineering process faster and cheaper | pursue |
| AI-based scheduling in own plants | 1 | Makes the core production process more efficient | explore |
| AI visual inspection as a machine option | 1 | A new feature on the existing product, for existing customers | explore |
| Predictive maintenance as a service | 2 | A new, recurring revenue stream and a different business model: selling uptime instead of machines | explore |
| Service copilot | 2 | Changes how service is delivered and could become a paid product | explore |
| Energy-optimised operating mode | 3 | Depends on customer demand and on evidence that does not exist yet | park |
| AI-supported 3D printing of parts | 3 | Depends on technology that is not yet in series use | park |

**What the horizon view adds to the ranking**

- **The only "pursue" is a Horizon 1 improvement.** That is not a coincidence. The scoring rewards feasibility, short time to value and low risk, and those are exactly the properties of Horizon 1. A weighted scoring model will tend to favour the core.
- **The strategy asks for Horizon 2.** Growing service revenue from 20% to 30% is a Horizon 2 goal, and the two Horizon 2 opportunities are both in "explore". If the company funds only what the ranking puts first, it improves engineering efficiency and makes no progress on its first strategic goal.
- **Both Horizon 2 opportunities hinge on the same unknown**: whether customers will share machine data and pay for a service. One round of customer conversations would inform both.
- **Horizon 3 is thin.** Two parked items are not a deliberate set of options; they are what was left over. A company that wants options would choose them on purpose, for example from the areas the [trend scan's discovery step](../trend-scans/2026-11_ai-in-industrial-manufacturing.md) found missing: security of connected factories, or AI in materials development.

**A recommendation that the ranking alone would not give:** fund the digital twin, and also fund the customer conversations for predictive maintenance out of a separate, smaller budget that is judged by what is learned, not by payback. Otherwise the portfolio serves one of three strategic goals.

## Limits of this example

- The horizon assignments are judgements, and the line between Horizon 1 and 2 is not sharp. AI visual inspection would be Horizon 2 if it were sold as a separate product to non-customers.
- The company is fictional and the scores are first-pass judgements.
- Seven opportunities are a small portfolio. The observation that scoring favours Horizon 1 is a tendency to watch for, not a proven rule.
